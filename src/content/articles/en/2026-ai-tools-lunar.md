## Imagine you have just landed on the moon

Getting chased around by "yet another new AI tool"? This piece does not hand you a shopping list. It gives you a way of thinking about tools: tools change, but what you are really building is your own work system.

Under your feet is a stretch of land nobody has worked yet. Your mission is simple: get settled, dig out something valuable, and make a life here. Start with whatever you have in your hands.

## 1. Start with the small shovel

![A settler on the moon digging out valuable ore with a hand-operated small shovel](/images/articles/2026-ai-tools-lunar/01-small-shovel.jpg)

For most people, the first tool is free. You open a chat window, ask a question, have it tidy up a paragraph, or look something up the way you would with a search engine.

That is the small shovel.

A small shovel is slow. You have to bend over, and every so often you stop to catch your breath. But do not underestimate it: if you know what you are digging for, a small shovel still brings up valuable ore. A summary of a client's email, a first draft of meeting notes, the outline of a proposal. You can start all of these with a free tool. A lot of people's first "hey, this is actually useful" moment comes from exactly this kind of small task.

So the first reminder: **free does not mean worthless, and you do not need to rush to prove you are "ready to upgrade."** Use what you have and finish one small task you genuinely need done. That matters more than researching which shovel is newest or shiniest. If the endless tool lists are making you anxious, start with [AI Using Tools On Its Own Is Becoming the Baseline—So You Don't Need to FOMO](/en/articles/dont-fomo-ai-tools/).

## 2. Switch to a power tool: the difference is the battery

![A small battery runs out, a larger one goes in, the power tool keeps digging, and on the right everything still needs a human check](/images/articles/2026-ai-tools-lunar/02-battery-journey.jpg)

As you keep digging, you notice that real work is never just one scoop.

Today there is a whole stack of data to sort out, tomorrow you need to compare three documents, and the day after you have to repeat the same process ten times. The small shovel is not incapable here; it just takes too much effort and gets interrupted too easily.

So some people switch to a power tool. A common way is a subscription, at about US$20 a month. A power tool lets you do more in the same amount of time, and people who have tried it often say the difference is obvious.

Here is the part that is easy to mix up, so let me be clear: **the battery is how long and how much you can use the tool, meaning quota and endurance. It is not how smart the tool is, and certainly not how accurate its answers are.**

Think of it this way. Same power tool, small battery: it dies halfway and the work stops midway. Swap in a bigger battery and you can finish. But how precisely it digs has nothing to do with battery size. You still have to hold the direction.

So when you catch yourself thinking "why did it stop again halfway," ask first: am I hitting a quota limit, or is this tool simply wrong for the job? The two have different answers and different fixes. The picture above shows the whole journey from left to right: the charge runs out, the battery gets swapped, the digging continues, and at the far right someone still has to pick up a magnifying glass and check.

## 3. Heavy machinery has a schedule: use the window to stock up raw material

![A large excavator on its scheduled run automatically sorting raw ore while the settler bags the material nearby](/images/articles/2026-ai-tools-lunar/03-excavator-promo.jpg)

Sometimes you hit a rare moment: a particular period is unusually good value, or you happen to have access to the strongest model. It is like a mine that occasionally gets a big machine brought in, available for only a limited time.

When that happens, my advice is: **if there is a window, use it quickly.**

But where you spend it matters. When a large excavator starts up, scoop after scoop of rock goes onto the sorting line. What it does is dig out raw ore in bulk, automatically, and do a first pass of sorting. What you get is **raw material**, not finished gold bars.

So what counts as raw material? It might be the way of asking that you only found after many tries. It might be a batch of tidy templates you can reuse later. It might be a first-hand feel for "this one is good at that, the other one fits this." These things stay with you after the window closes.

The flip side: if you just leave the machine running and watch, without picking up and saving anything useful, then when the window closes all you have left is the memory of "it seemed amazing back then."

## 4. What you are really building is your own base

![The settler standing in front of a moon base, with a robot, supply bags of tools, and a skill path beside them](/images/articles/2026-ai-tools-lunar/04-pioneer-base.jpg)

At this point I want to turn the conversation away from "tools" a little.

When people hear "accumulate," many think of knowledge management first: sort everything into categories and make it all look tidy. I do not recommend going in that direction. **What you are building is not a filing cabinet. It is a working environment that actually runs, a work system of your own.**

What is in this base?

- **Your Skills.** Fix the workflows that go smoothly so you do not have to explain from scratch next time. If the idea is new to you, see [A Skill Is Really the Evolution of GPTs](/en/articles/from-prompt-to-skill/).
- **The toolset you use every day.** Not many, but every piece sits where your hand falls naturally.
- **An understanding of each model and feature.** Who is good at writing, who is good at looking things up, who suits data work, what you hand over and what you keep to yourself. This is soft knowledge. You cannot copy it from a book; you can only get it by using.

Look at the settler in the picture: a base behind them, sorted supply bags and tool modules at their feet, and a skill path growing upward beside them. The point is not how much stuff there is, but that it is all connected, so you can walk out the door and start working every day.

The small shovel, the power tool, and the heavy machinery are all in the end there to add bricks to this base. Tools change and models update, but the habits, workflows, and judgment you build up in the base go with you.

## 5. The final step: pick up the magnifying glass

![The settler checking ore with a magnifying glass, with unverified raw ore and refined results on the workbench](/images/articles/2026-ai-tools-lunar/05-magnifier-refine.jpg)

With all that said, here is the most important thing.

**Paying does not automatically give you correct answers, and it does not automatically turn into gold.**

Whether you are using the small shovel or the biggest excavator, what comes out is raw ore. Raw ore has gold in it, and stones, and things that look a lot like gold but are not. Whether it becomes a result depends on this last step: a person.

The step is plain: pick up the magnifying glass.

Are the numbers right? Does the citation have a source? Is this sentence appropriate in front of the client? Did we miss a premise that only you know? Set the doubtful things aside, and move them into the final product only after you have confirmed them. Only you can do this refining, and only you should be responsible for it.

You may feel that this is a bit tiring. But look at it another way: the machine has taken on the hardest part of mining, so your time can go into judging, choosing, and revising, which is where the real value is.

## Back to your Monday

If you want to compress this piece into a few lines:

- A free tool is a small shovel. Slow, but it digs up real things. Start there.
- A subscription is a power tool. The battery is quota and endurance, not accuracy.
- A rare window is heavy machinery. Use it to stock up raw material, not just for the spectacle.
- What you are really building is your own working environment: Skills, a handy toolset, and an understanding of each model.
- Paying does not guarantee gold. In the end, someone has to hold the magnifying glass.

So this Monday, you do not need to rush to decide what to buy. Pick one thing you actually need to do, run it through the tools you already have, keep something that you can reuse, and then look over the result with your magnifying glass.

That is how a base gets built, one brick at a time.
